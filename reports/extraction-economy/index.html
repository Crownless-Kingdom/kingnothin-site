Opening record

THE QUIET TRANSACTION

The morning begins with a hand reaching for a phone.

The weather is checked. A message is opened. A route is searched. A prescription price is compared. Nothing about the moment feels historic. It feels like Tuesday.

Behind the glass, those small acts can become records: a location, a query, a pause, a purchase, a device identifier, an inferred interest. One record carries little meaning. Thousands of records, joined over time and matched with information from other places, can describe a person with startling intimacy.

This is the Extraction Economy: the system that turns human life into profiles, predictions, attention, influence, and profit.

The system has many uses and many participants. It includes social platforms, advertising exchanges, data brokers, retailers, app developers, analytics companies, artificial-intelligence developers, and the technical intermediaries most people never encounter by name. Each performs a different function. Together, they have built markets around the observation and classification of ordinary behaviour.

The Federal Trade Commission's 2024 examination of nine major social-media and video-streaming companies found extensive collection involving users and, in some cases, non-users. The companies gathered information from their own services, outside sources, data brokers, tracking technologies, and corporate affiliates. Personal information fed advertising systems, recommendation engines, analytics, and AI. Several companies struggled or declined to provide a complete account of what they collected or where it went. [1]

The imbalance begins there.

The person sees a screen. The system sees a developing record.

Core narrative

I. THE NEW SURVEILLANCE ORDER

Shoshana Zuboff gave one influential account of this arrangement the name surveillance capitalism. Her framework describes a commercial logic in which experience becomes behavioural data, and behavioural data becomes material for prediction. It is a theory of economic power, widely used and also debated. The documented practices beneath it are concrete: large-scale collection, inference, targeting, automated ranking, and markets built around access to people and their probable actions. [2]

Advertising supplied much of the original commercial engine. Meta told investors that substantially all of its 2025 revenue came from advertising placements. Its annual filing also says its targeting and measurement tools use signals generated inside its products and from activity on external websites and services beyond its control. Alphabet reported $294.7 billion in Google advertising revenue for 2025. These filings describe a simple incentive with enormous consequences: attention creates advertising inventory, and information can make that inventory more valuable. [3] [4]

Artificial intelligence adds another demand for data, though the relationship deserves precision. AI systems use many kinds of training material: licensed collections, public web content, code, images, scientific records, purpose-built datasets, synthetic examples, user interactions, and—in some circumstances—personal information. Every AI system carries its own provenance.

Canada supplied a revealing case in May 2026. Federal and provincial privacy authorities concluded a joint investigation into OpenAI's development and deployment of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. They found the issues under investigation well-founded and conditionally resolved after commitments addressing data minimization, consent, notice, access, correction, and the treatment of personal information in training data and user interactions. The decision concerns specific systems under Canadian law. Its wider significance lies in the mechanism it exposes: information gathered for one context can enter the development of systems serving another. [5]

The National Telecommunications and Information Administration has identified training-data provenance, collection, curation, management, and disclosure as central elements of AI accountability. It has also identified overcollection of personal information for training as a privacy risk. [6]

The Extraction Economy therefore reaches AI through evidence, one system at a time. The strongest account follows the data rather than treating every algorithm as surveillance.

Core mechanism

II. SEVEN LAYERS OF EXTRACTION

The following seven layers form a KINGNOTHIN explanatory model. They describe a common progression assembled from regulatory findings, technical standards, company disclosures, and enforcement records. A service may use several layers, skip others, or combine them inside one operation.

One life. Seven transformations.

01 YOU GIVE

What You Give

An account begins with volunteered information: a name, email address, birthday, photograph, post, message, search, form entry, uploaded contact list, or spoken prompt.

The exchange feels direct because the person performs the act. The future uses can reach far beyond the original moment. Purpose limitation asks a basic question: does information collected for one reason remain confined to that reason?

Canadian privacy law requires organizations to identify purposes, limit collection, and limit subsequent use, disclosure, and retention. The European Union's GDPR establishes related duties of purpose limitation and data minimization. These principles address the first layer before volume and complexity make control harder. [7] [8]

02 YOU DO

What You Do

Behavioural data records activity rather than biography: pages viewed, links selected, videos watched, searches entered, time spent, purchases made, items abandoned, and patterns of return.

The FTC found that major platforms used such information for advertising, engagement measurement, recommendations, product decisions, analytics, and automated systems. Behaviour becomes useful because repetition creates shape. A single pause says little. A pattern of pauses can become an interest category. [1]

03 DEVICE REVEALS

What the Device Reveals

Apps and devices can produce location data, advertising identifiers, IP addresses, sensor information, crash reports, and other telemetry. Some collection supports security or core functionality. Some extends into advertising and profiling.

Software development kits make this layer especially difficult to see. An app developer can include another company's code for analytics, advertising, mapping, or other services. The FTC alleged that X-Mode and InMarket used SDKs embedded in apps to collect precise location data. InMarket's consumer-facing permission messages described store reminders and rewards while the company allegedly combined location information with other sources to construct advertising profiles. [9]

04 TRACKING FOLLOWS

What Follows You

Cookies, tracking pixels, account identifiers, and browser fingerprints can recognize activity across pages, sessions, or services.

Pixels may consist of embedded HTML or JavaScript capable of transmitting information about a visit or action. FTC cases involving GoodRx and BetterHelp alleged that health-related platforms disclosed user information to advertising companies through pixels and related tools. [10]

Browser fingerprinting takes another route. It combines observable characteristics—browser version, language, time zone, screen dimensions, fonts, codecs, settings, and other signals—into a potentially distinguishing pattern. The World Wide Web Consortium warns that fingerprinting can correlate activity across sites and sessions without clear user controls, including situations where cookie restrictions provide little protection. [11]

Ordinary moment

THE 2:13 A.M. SEARCH

At 2:13 in the morning, someone types a medical symptom into a search box. The words become observed behaviour at Layer 2. A health page opened from the results may contain an advertising pixel that sends information about the visit and an identifier to an advertising provider at Layer 4. If an intermediary connects that identifier with information from other sources, Layer 5 can add the visit to a broader profile. Layers 6 and 7 can then turn the pattern into an inferred health interest and use it to shape a later advertisement or recommendation. FTC cases involving GoodRx and BetterHelp document parts of this chain. The exact route depends on the services, code, settings, and data relationships involved in that particular visit. [10] [12]

05 OTHERS ADD

What Others Add

Data brokers and aggregators join information collected from different sources. Commercial records, public records, transactions, online activity, location feeds, and purchased datasets can be matched or appended to an existing profile.

The FTC's 2014 data-broker study found extensive collection across online and offline sources, circulation among brokers, and the creation of consumer segments and sensitive inferences. Seven of the nine brokers studied had shared information with another broker in the same study. The agency described a market that operated largely beyond ordinary public visibility. [12]

06 SYSTEM INFERS

What the System Infers

An inference is a conclusion drawn from other information. It may concern age, income, health, religion, political interest, purchasing power, family status, or probable intent.

An inference can be wrong and still acquire power. Once a category enters an advertising, fraud, recommendation, eligibility, or pricing system, the person may experience its consequence without ever seeing its name.

The FTC documented brokers creating potentially sensitive categories and platforms using algorithms to infer personal and demographic information about users and non-users. The profile becomes more than a record of past behaviour. It becomes a proposition about the person. [1] [12]

Ordinary moment

THE QUIET GROCERY RUN

A shopper opens a loyalty app beneath the white lights of a grocery aisle. The account information belongs to Layer 1. Searches, clipped coupons, abandoned items, and purchases become Layer 2. Location access or an embedded SDK can contribute Layer 3 signals. Information acquired from brokers or other partners can add household, demographic, or purchasing categories at Layer 5. Layer 6 may infer price sensitivity, family status, or likely demand. At Layer 7, those conclusions can influence the promotion, product order, or price placed before the shopper. FTC staff found that pricing intermediaries can use customer characteristics, interactions, purchases, perceived loyalty, and willingness-to-pay signals in targeting tools. The agency's published examples remain illustrative because confidential business information limited public disclosure. [9] [12] [14]

07 PROFILE ACTS

What the Profile Changes

The final layer activates the profile. It influences the advertisement, recommendation, search result, promotion, price, risk signal, or piece of content placed before someone.

Real-time bidding provides one documented mechanism. When an ad space becomes available, an exchange can conduct an automated auction in milliseconds. Information about the page, device, location, or audience may travel to prospective bidders. The FTC's Mobilewalla case alleged that the company collected and retained information from auctions it had lost, including sensitive location information. [13]

Pricing technology extends the same logic toward commerce. In a 2025 interim study, FTC staff found that intermediary companies offered tools capable of using direct, inferred, first-party, and third-party information to shape prices, promotions, product rankings, and offers. The report used hypothetical illustrations to protect confidential business information, and two commissioners dissented from its release. The evidence establishes capability and reported practice within the examined market. It leaves the prevalence and consumer effects open for further study. [14]

Ordinary moment

THE TEN-MINUTE STOP

A person drives across town and leaves the phone in a coat pocket while visiting a clinic, a place of worship, a union hall, or a political gathering. An app with location access can collect coordinates and a mobile advertising identifier at Layer 3. A broker or aggregator can receive, purchase, or combine that feed at Layer 5. The resulting location pattern can become an audience segment or enter the data transmitted through an advertising auction at Layer 7. FTC actions involving X-Mode, InMarket, and Mobilewalla document versions of these mechanisms and the sensitivity of the places they can reveal. The person experiences a ten-minute stop. The system may retain a timestamped proposition about identity. [9] [13]

The seven layers produce a causal chain:

signal → profile → prediction → decision → retained value

The person supplies the life. The system determines the commercial meaning.

Research layer

III. THE MARKET FOR PREDICTION

The phrase “companies sell your data” describes several different transactions.

Large consumer platforms commonly sell advertising placements and targeting capabilities. Advertisers choose an audience or objective; the platform selects and delivers the advertisement using information and models inside its environment. The advertiser usually receives performance reporting rather than a copy of the platform's entire user database.

Data brokers occupy another part of the market. They can sell, license, disclose, or provide access to datasets, segments, scores, and derived products. Advertising technology adds further intermediaries: publishers, publisher ad servers, supply-side platforms, exchanges, demand-side platforms, measurement firms, and advertisers.

Google's position across the open-web advertising stack became the subject of a federal antitrust case. In April 2025, the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia found that Google held monopoly power in markets for publisher ad servers and ad exchanges and had unlawfully tied products and entrenched that power through anticompetitive conduct. The decision concerns competition law. It also shows how control over technical infrastructure can concentrate information, customers, and bargaining power. [15]

Economic benefit deserves equal care. Behavioural advertising can improve relevance and help some merchants reach potential customers. Its distribution of value remains contested.

Two online experiments by Eduardo Schnadower Mustri, Idris Adjerid, and Alessandro Acquisti found that behaviourally targeted advertisements were more relevant than random products, while the advertised products were associated with higher prices and lower-quality vendors than competing search results in their study settings. A separate empirical study by Veronica Marotta, Vibhanshu Abhishek, and Acquisti estimated that the availability of a user's cookie increased publisher revenue by roughly four percent in the dataset examined. Each study has a defined design and scope. Together, they weaken claims that pervasive tracking delivers large, automatic benefits to every participant. [16] [17]

The market thrives on precision. Its public justification often rests on relevance. Its deeper asset is the capacity to classify, reach, and test people at scale.

Research layer

IV. THE PRIVACY PROMISE

Privacy language has commercial value. A shield icon, a private mode, a safety claim, or an “anonymous” label can reassure a person long enough for the transaction to continue.

The term privacy washing describes the gap between a privacy promise and the underlying practice. A factual report proves that gap case by case.

Avast provides a documented example. In 2024, the FTC finalized an order resolving allegations that the company collected detailed browsing information through antivirus software and browser extensions, stored it in granular form, and sold it through a subsidiary after presenting its products as protection from online tracking. The order banned the sale or licensing of browsing data for advertising, required deletion of transferred information and derived products, imposed a privacy program, and required a $16.5 million payment. [18]

GoodRx provides another. The FTC alleged that the company disclosed sensitive health information to Facebook, Google, and others and misrepresented aspects of its privacy and HIPAA practices. The resulting order placed limits on advertising disclosures and required other remedies. [19]

These cases establish conduct by named companies under defined legal proceedings. They also reveal a recurring design hazard: the public-facing promise can be simpler than the data operation behind it.

Privacy depends on the operation.

What enters. Who receives it. How long it remains. What new purpose appears later. What decision it changes.

Research layer

V. THE REGULATORY FRONT

Privacy law arrives as a map drawn over moving machinery.

United States

The United States uses a patchwork of sectoral federal statutes, FTC enforcement, state privacy laws, state attorneys general, private rights of action in defined areas, and competition law. This structure has produced meaningful orders and judgments. It also creates gaps, exemptions, uneven rights, and different rules across jurisdictions.

Massachusetts illustrates both momentum and uncertainty. The state Senate passed S.2619 unanimously in September 2025. The House passed a substantially amended version unanimously on June 4, 2026. The Senate declined to concur on June 11, and a conference committee was appointed. That committee held public meetings in July. The official legislative history reviewed through August 29 records an active negotiation rather than an enacted final law. [20]

The strongest principle in the debate is also the plainest: collect what the service requires, use it for the stated purpose, retain it for a defined period, and release it under accountable rules.

European Union

The GDPR makes data minimization and purpose limitation legal principles. Personal data must be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for the stated purpose. The regulation also establishes rights and duties involving access, correction, erasure, objection, security, accountability, and lawful processing. Its real-world record includes enforcement, litigation, compliance investment, institutional variation, and continuing disputes over interpretation. [8]

Canada

Canada's federal private-sector framework remains PIPEDA. It requires accountability, identified purposes, consent, limited collection, limited use and retention, accuracy, safeguards, openness, individual access, and a process for challenging compliance. Alberta, British Columbia, and Quebec apply substantially similar provincial private-sector laws within their respective scope. [7]

Bill C-27, which proposed a replacement federal privacy statute and an artificial-intelligence law, died on the Order Paper when Parliament was prorogued in January 2025. Canada's existing laws continued to govern. The 2026 OpenAI findings demonstrate how those laws can reach model training, web-sourced personal information, licensed datasets, and user interactions. They also show the limits of an older framework confronting systems built at global scale. [5] [21]

Across these jurisdictions, data minimization changes the starting question. The familiar question asks whether a person clicked “agree.” The stronger question asks why the information entered the system at all.

Research layer

VI. THE LABOUR OF RESISTANCE

Policy rarely arrives by itself. Someone reads the filing, tests the app, interviews the witness, drafts the amendment, explains the mechanism, and stays in the room while better-funded interests explain why the machinery must remain exactly as it is.

A 2021 study by Jennifer Holt and Lisa Parks examined this work through interviews conducted in 2018 with ten staff members at six Washington-based advocacy organizations, plus five other experts and researchers. The authors identified four recurring forms of labour: building coalitions, setting agendas, navigating relationships with companies and government, and balancing domestic with global policy. [22]

The study offers a valuable close view with a defined boundary. Its scope covers a small professional community at a particular moment: the interviewed participants, their organizations, and their policy environment.

Its details matter. Advocates described testing apps, reading terms, employing technologists, negotiating access to company information, working around nondisclosure agreements, building legal challenges, and translating technical conduct into public language. Their proximity to companies created both access and tension. Staff movement between advocacy groups and industry could remove institutional memory from one organization while carrying public-interest expertise into another.

The evidence supports vigilance toward capture. It also demands proof before the label is applied to a particular organization or person.

Resistance is work. Its strength depends on funding, technical knowledge, legal authority, public trust, and the ability to remain independent while standing close enough to inspect the machine.

Research layer

VII. FROM INDIVIDUAL CONSENT TO COLLECTIVE TERMS

The standard privacy interface isolates the person.

One screen. One policy. One button. One individual facing an institution with lawyers, engineers, servers, and time.

Collective data governance begins from a different arrangement. A community, profession, tribe, cooperative, or group establishes terms for access, use, benefit, and accountability. The forms include data cooperatives, trusts, commons, repositories, licenses, and stewardship organizations. Each carries its own legal structure and risk. [23]

Mozilla Data Collective is one young example. Launched in 2025 and moved into a standalone mission-led entity in 2026, the platform says dataset providers can control access, define permitted uses, set prices, and retain their rights. Mozilla reported more than 190 organizations sharing over 600 datasets in more than 300 languages by May 2026. These figures and claims come from the organization itself. Long-term evidence about enforcement, sustainability, benefit distribution, and scale is still developing. [24]

The Nwulite Obodo Open Data Licence offers another model. Developed in 2024 and documented by Chijioke Okorie and Melissa Omino, NOODL uses differentiated terms intended to address unequal bargaining power between African dataset holders and better-resourced users. It treats openness as a relationship with conditions rather than a permanent surrender of value. Its design is documented; its long-term record remains young. [25]

Native BioData Consortium moves the question onto sovereign ground. NativeBio describes itself as an Indigenous-led nonprofit repository and research centre located on the Cheyenne River Sioux Reservation. Its governance centres tribal consent, ownership, benefit, jurisdiction, and control over genetic, environmental, health, and social data. Here, data sovereignty means authority exercised by a people, through institutions built to serve that people. [26]

Rest of World has reported further experiments: language communities in Pakistan using access conditions for hundreds of hours of speech data; the Kerala Food Platform serving roughly 2,500 farmers; and cooperatives that help producers govern records tied to their work. These are reported examples rather than proof of a settled model. The same report identifies the hard questions: governance, scale, enforcement, and the danger that a cooperative created to protect data becomes dependent on monetizing it. [27]

Collective governance changes the posture of the exchange.

The community arrives with terms.

Core narrative

VIII. WHAT THE EXTRACTION COSTS

Privacy loss is often described as exposure. The larger cost is altered power.

A profile can decide which offer appears first. A location trail can reveal a visit to a clinic, a church, a union hall, or a protest. A browsing record can disclose a private concern before the person has spoken it aloud. An inference can classify someone incorrectly and still shape the system around them. A recommendation engine can reorder attention one item at a time.

The decisive record often remains invisible to the person it describes.

That opacity limits correction. It also changes the terms of participation. Daily life increasingly passes through systems that observe first and explain later.

The Extraction Economy grows through ordinary convenience. Its power comes from accumulation: more moments, more links, more confidence, more uses.

Clarity begins by separating the chain.

  • What information enters?
  • Which actor collects it?
  • Which other actor receives it?
  • What does the system infer?
  • Which decision changes?
  • Who retains the value?
  • Which right, tool, or institution can interrupt the process?

These questions turn atmosphere into mechanism.

Core narrative

IX. THE PRACTICAL EXIT

No individual setting can reform an entire market. Individual action still matters because exposure accumulates through particular devices, accounts, permissions, brokers, and habits.

A practical first response begins close to home:

  1. Review location, microphone, camera, contacts, photo, and background permissions on the phone.
  2. Remove apps whose access exceeds their practical purpose.
  3. Use browser protections that restrict cross-site tracking and fingerprinting.
  4. Separate sensitive searches and accounts from routine browsing where practical.
  5. Review advertising, activity, and model-training controls in major accounts.
  6. Submit deletion or access requests where law and service controls provide them.
  7. Support institutions pursuing data minimization, enforceable rights, independent research, and collective governance.

The larger exit is structural: limited collection, purpose-bound use, short retention, accountable sharing, auditable systems, effective remedies, and collective bargaining power.

Privacy is the foundation. Agency is the goal. Wisdom is the destination.

The machine becomes less mysterious when its parts acquire names.

The person remains more than the profile.
